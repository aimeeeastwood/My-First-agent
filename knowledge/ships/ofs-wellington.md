# OFS Wellington (CVA-3)

## Canon Status
Active Service Vessel (2061 — Battle of Kanaky period)
Fast Attack Carrier (Canberra-class)
Third and final hull of Canberra-class production line

---

## Registry Designation
- **CVA-3 — OFS Wellington**
- Canberra-class Fast Attack Carrier (FAC)
- Final operational unit of Canberra-class doctrine

---

## Class Context

The Canberra-class represents the Oceanic Federation Navy’s first Fast Attack Carrier doctrine:
- distributed maritime warfare theory
- high-tempo strike operations
- mid-size carrier balance between presence and agility

Wellington is the **final iteration of this class**, incorporating all operational refinements developed during earlier Canberra-class deployments.

---

## Operational Role (2061)

At the onset of the Battle of Kanaky, OFS Wellington is an active frontline carrier deployed in Melanesian theatre operations.

Primary responsibilities include:
- forward air-maritime strike coordination
- support of OFN task group operations
- sea lane security across vulnerable Federation logistics corridors
- rapid response engagement in island-chain warfare environments

---

## Doctrinal Position

Wellington operates at the intersection of two evolving OFN doctrines:

### 1. Canberra-class doctrine (legacy)
- Fast Attack Carrier theory
- medium-sized carrier deployment groups
- emphasis on distributed engagement

### 2. Emerging wartime doctrine (Kuala Lumpur-class era)
- stealth-first carrier design
- survivability over presence
- reduced visibility operational strategy

Wellington is therefore considered:

> the final operational expression of pre-Kanaky carrier doctrine

---

## Strategic Importance in Battle of Kanaky

The Battle of Kanaky represents the first major conflict in which:
- Canberra-class carriers are fully committed
- Kuala Lumpur-class carriers are still under construction
- OFN doctrine is actively under revision during combat operations

Within this environment, Wellington functions as:
- a frontline strike carrier
- a command-support vessel in contested Melanesian waters
- a stabilising asset within rapidly evolving fleet doctrine

---

## Historical Note (Non-Canonical at Time of Service)

At the time of the Battle of Kanaky, Wellington is not considered a legend or symbolic vessel.

Her historical significance:
- is not yet established
- will be determined retrospectively after the war
- becomes prominent only through later historiography of the Second Asia-Pacific War

## Cultural Status (2061)

## Summary

OFS Wellington (CVA-3) is the final Canberra-class Fast Attack Carrier and serves as a frontline asset during the Battle of Kanaky.

She represents:

> the end of one naval doctrine and the starting point of another

## 🧭 Crew Culture & Command Relations (2061 — Early Emergence)

During early deployment in the Battle of Kanaky, OFS Wellington develops a distinctive internal operational culture.

The crew is widely regarded within OFN operational circles as:
- highly professional
- tactically autonomous in execution
- unusually resistant to rigid command interpretation during fast-moving engagements

This has led to informal tension between shipboard command culture and certain higher-level OFN operational planners.

---

## ⚠️ Command Perception

Within segments of OFN command structure, particularly under Admiral Thompson’s operational doctrine, Wellington is occasionally characterised as:

> “an undisciplined or excessively autonomous combat platform”

and, in informal briefing contexts:

> “a rogue-capable vessel with an overconfident crew culture”

These characterisations are not formal doctrine assessments, but they reflect internal friction between command philosophy and battlefield execution.

---

## 🧩 Crew Perspective

Conversely, Wellington’s crew culture emphasises:
- operational independence in contested environments
- rapid adaptation under fire
- strong internal cohesion under high-pressure engagements

This has resulted in mutual scepticism between shipboard personnel and certain command elements.

---

## 📌 Cultural Note (Emerging Pattern)

At this stage in the war, no formal “Wellington reputation mythos” exists.

However, repeated exposure to high-intensity engagements is beginning to generate:
- informal fleet stories
- competing interpretations of the ship’s conduct
- early-stage reputation divergence between command and frontline personnel

These narratives will later become significant in post-war historiography.

## 🪖 Battlefield Reputation — Battle of Kanaky (2061)

During the Battle of Kanaky, OFS Wellington develops a strong positive reputation among deployed OFN personnel, allied units, and attached support elements operating in the Melanesian theatre.

Within frontline contexts, the ship is widely regarded as:

- reliable under extreme operational pressure
- highly responsive in contested air-maritime conditions
- unusually effective in coordinating rapid strike cycles during fluid engagements

---

## 👥 Crew Leadership Perception (“Mum”)

The ship’s command leadership, referred to informally by personnel as “Mum,” is regarded positively by embedded and attached forces operating alongside Wellington.

Common frontline sentiment includes:
- strong protective leadership under fire
- consistent prioritisation of crew survival during high-risk operations
- willingness to deviate from rigid doctrine when battlefield conditions demand it

---

## ⚖️ Command vs Battlefield Divergence

A distinction emerges during Kanaky between:
- strategic-level OFN command interpretations of operational behaviour
- and frontline lived experience of Wellington’s effectiveness

While higher command discussions may describe Wellington in terms of doctrinal deviation, this does not reflect battlefield sentiment within the Kanaky theatre.

---

## 📌 Summary

Within the Battle of Kanaky operational environment:

> OFS Wellington is regarded as a highly capable and respected carrier platform, with strong positive crew leadership reputation and high frontline trust.

Negative or “rogue ship” characterisations do not originate from Kanaky theatre personnel.
