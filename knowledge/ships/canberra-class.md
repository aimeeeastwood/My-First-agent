KAIROS CANON ARCHIVE ENTRY

TITLE: Canberra-class Fast Attack Carrier (FAC)
ERA: Pre–Second Asia-Pacific War / Early OFN Militarisation (2050–2061)
STATUS: ACTIVE DURING EARLY WAR / PHASED OUT DURING CONFLICT EVOLUTION
CLASSIFICATION: Fast Attack Carrier (Experimental Transitional Doctrine)

⚙️ 1. CORE DESIGN IDEA

The Canberra-class is not a fleet carrier.

It is a Fast Attack Carrier (FAC) — a hybrid doctrine vessel designed for:

rapid strike deployment
short-to-mid range carrier warfare
distributed task force operations
high-tempo engagement cycles

At ~300 metres, it sits in a deliberate middle ground:

large enough to project power
small enough to evade traditional fleet tracking assumptions

🧠 2. DOCTRINAL CONTEXT (CRITICAL)

The Canberra-class emerges from a theoretical phase of OFN naval thinking:

“Future naval warfare will be distributed, fast, and networked.”

The Federation anticipates:

fragmented engagements
dispersed carrier groups
drone-heavy strike packages
sensor-first combat
reduced importance of single supercarriers

So instead of building one dominant fleet carrier doctrine, they trial:

multiple smaller, faster, semi-independent carriers

🌊 3. WHY 300 METRES MATTER

That size is intentional compromise:

large enough for sustained air operations
small enough for faster repositioning
easier to conceal within task group dispersion
cheaper and faster to produce than fleet carriers
designed for “hit and reposition” doctrine

They are essentially:

carriers built for contact warfare, not presence warfare

⚔️ 4. ROLE IN EARLY WAR (2055–2061)

When the Cleansing Light conflict begins escalating:

Canberra-class ships are used for:

rapid response strikes
South China Sea interdiction
Melanesian island hopping operations
initial Kanaky support deployments
containment operations in Vietnam theatre

But combat reality diverges from doctrine:

engagements become larger than expected
shore-based missile density increases
airspace denial expands rapidly
survivability becomes the limiting factor
💥 5. THE KANAKY TURNING POINT

The Battle of Kanaky becomes doctrinal rupture.

Key observation:

even “fast attack carriers” are still too visible, too large, too targetable

The lesson is not simply “they were used wrong”.

It becomes a structural conclusion:

FACs are not agile enough for this threat environment
dispersal is insufficient without stealth dominance
survivability must be designed in, not assumed
🔄 6. DOCTRINAL SPLIT AFTER KANAKY

This is where your major evolution happens:

Old belief (pre-Kanaky)

Smaller carriers = better survivability

War reality (Kanaky + Melanesia)

Smaller carriers are still large targets in a missile-saturated theatre

So OFN splits thinking:

faction 1: “deployment error, not design failure” (Thompson argument)
faction 2: “platform design is fundamentally obsolete”
🚀 7. SUCCESSOR PATH → KUALA LUMPUR-CLASS

The war forces a pivot:

Canberra-class → deemed insufficient for survivability envelope
→ leads to full redesign philosophy:

smaller hulls
reduced radar cross section
higher speed emphasis
stealth-first doctrine
distributed strike reliance

That becomes:

Kuala Lumpur-class fast stealth carriers

🧩 8. RELATION TO WELLINGTON

This is important for your narrative hierarchy:

Canberra-class = doctrinal experiment
Wellington = operational myth vessel
Kuala Lumpur-class = post-trauma adaptation

So Wellington sits between theory and evolution:

She is the ship that proved the experiment was real.

🧾 9. IN-UNIVERSE SUMMARY

By 2063 historians generally describe Canberra-class carriers as:

“The OFN’s first attempt at post-fleet carrier warfare, overtaken by the speed and scale of Second Asia-Pacific War escalation.”
